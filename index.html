01 / Research Proposal

Risk-conditioned whole-body navigation

Two learned policies run on the robot: a contact planner and a whole-body controller.

02 / Background

Contact planning connects terrain to whole-body motion

Observe the terrain

Estimate available surfaces from onboard perception.

→
Plan body contacts

Choose where feet, hands, and other body parts should contact.

→
Control the whole body

Coordinate joint actions to execute the contacts.

Existing capability

ACP demonstrates this hierarchy for diverse humanoid behaviors. Motion data initializes learning; the deployed policies operate without reference motions.

Our research questions

How should measured foot support change contact execution? How should a user’s risk preference change the robot’s next contact choices?

Terrain geometry describes possible contacts. Tactile feedback provides evidence about actual support after contact.

ACP · TactileStep · Tac4Loco. We study tactile feedback and adjustable risk within this contact-planning framework.

04 / Problem Formulation

Reach a goal under uncertain contact conditions

Command

Goal position, goal heading, risk setting

→
Robot observations

Terrain geometry, robot state, tactile history

→
Navigation

Choose contacts and execute joint actions

Goal command

Target base position: horizontal location and height, plus heading. This is the same command for walking, crawling, and climbing.

Risk command

A scalar controls how much poor outcomes matter. The robot may take a detour, add hand support, or use a faster crossing.

Test different risk settings on the same start, goal, and terrain.

Battery state, if used, is a separate physical observation. A risk setting expresses preference; it does not identify friction or battery capacity.

06 / Contact Command

Specify the next contact for each body

Representation

For each hand, foot, knee, elbow, and the pelvis: one XYZ target and one active flag. Start with a 1.5-second look-ahead horizon.

Coordinate frame

The origin is the robot base. Vertical stays aligned with gravity; horizontal axes follow the base heading. An inactive target is masked.

The planner commands upcoming contact points. Dense force/torque sensing feeds back actual contact loads to the controller.

07 / Dense Six-Axis Tactile Feedback

Dense force and torque feedback

What the policy observes

Spatial patterns of normal force, tangential force (shear), and local torque. Combine them with robot motion to learn contact recovery.

What we test

Do these signals improve recovery from sliding, foot twisting, and partial support beyond pressure maps or one total force/torque measurement?

Force and torque together are called a wrench. Slip velocity, friction limits, and terrain material still require inference. Only instrumented body parts provide these measurements. Force/torque frame conventions.

08 / Stage1 Contact Controller Training

Learn contact execution and recovery

Paired training data

G1 motion + aligned terrain + contact labels

→
Train controller with RL

Reference contacts, body motion, and disturbances

→
Test without motion input

Controller receives deployable observations and contact targets

Training objectives

Track active contact targets, establish intended support, stabilize the base, and limit joint effort and impacts. Use motion tracking to initialize coordination.

Recovery curriculum

Vary friction, foothold edges, pushes, sensor delay, and force/torque noise. Introduce compliant supports after rigid-contact recovery is reliable.

The deployed actor receives no reference joint trajectory. Reference motion contributes training rewards and initialization.

Audit motion–terrain alignment first. Use simulation contact measurements to verify labels; a kinematic motion preview does not establish dynamic feasibility.

09 / Stage2 Planner Initialization

Learn targets from contacts the controller achieves

Run the controller

Start on the paired reference terrains

→
Keep successful episodes

Record actual body–terrain contact onsets

→
Fit the contact planner

Predict next contact positions and active flags

Where supervision comes from

Motion clips initialize controller training. Planner labels come from the controller’s successful executions, found by looking forward in each recorded episode.

Prediction and transition

Predict an active flag and a terrain-point distribution for each body. Gradually replace reference contacts with predicted contacts during controller training.

A successful rollout supplies feasible examples. It does not estimate how often the same plan fails.

ACP uses successful controller rollouts for hindsight contact labels. Its reference sources also include AME-policy motion. These are different roles. ACP paper, training and hindsight labeling.

10 / Stage3 Risk-Conditioned Planning

Learn contact choices from navigation outcomes

Sample a task

Terrain, start, goal, risk setting, physical variation

→
Execute the hierarchy

Planner samples contacts; controller drives G1

→
Update the planner

Use successful and failed navigation episodes

Planner actions and return

Sample a terrain target and active flag per body. Reward goal progress and completion; penalize elapsed time, falls, collisions, and excessive effort.

Training schedule

Freeze the controller first to isolate planner learning. Then alternate planner updates with controller adaptation; recompute risk estimates after controller changes.

Risk conditions both the planner input and its RL objective. A distributional critic alone does not create controllable behavior.

Use a stochastic terrain-point selector during RL so action probabilities are available. Keep a small amount of successful-contact supervision only if it helps initialization.

11 / Risk Objective

CVaR gives more weight to poor outcomes

Conditional value at risk (CVaR) here means the average of the worst α fraction of episode returns. Return is the total navigation reward; larger is better.

10%: conservative · 100%: mean return

Train over several α values; keep α fixed within each training episode. Start with empirical episodic CVaR and compare a distributional critic. Lower-tail CVaR covers risk-averse through risk-neutral behavior, not explicit risk-seeking.

12 / Execution and Feedback

Respond to contact loss before choosing the next step

1 · Observe support

Local forces and torques change; robot motion changes

→
2 · Correct locally

Controller shifts load or replaces the foot

→
3 · Replan contacts

Planner uses the new observations and risk setting

What tactile history can change

Test whether force/torque history improves the next foothold or hand-support choice. Evaluate this separately from immediate controller recovery.

Risk changes during execution

Test command changes during stable support first. Preserve controller state and limit contact-command jumps; then test changes during transitions.

Hypothesis: tactile feedback improves execution, and the planner uses that feedback to make better subsequent decisions.

Force/torque history does not uniquely identify terrain material. Hidden friction and compliance are randomized; the deployed actor must infer useful consequences from available observations.

Procedural goal-reaching training uses no matched motion clip for each scene. Motion data initializes capability; RL supplies new experience.

Use unseen terrain seeds, dimensions, and contact parameters in evaluation. Local contact planning does not solve arbitrary maze navigation; begin with locally observable route alternatives.

14 / Evaluation Metrics

Measure recovery, risk, and contact strategy

QuestionMeasurement
Does tactile feedback help?Active-body contact-position error; stance foot slip relative to the support surface; recovery time; fall rate
Does risk change behavior?Same-task sweep of α: route choice, hand-support frequency, completion time, success rate
Are poor outcomes improved?Empirical lower-tail return and failure rate across repeated stochastic trials, with confidence intervals
Does sensing transfer?Six-axis calibration, cross-axis coupling, delay, failed sensing elements; simulation-to-hardware performance change
Does the system generalize?Success and recovery on withheld geometry, materials, and obstacle compositions

For contact-position error, compare the measured body contact point with its commanded terrain target at contact onset. For deforming terrain, use local surface motion as the slip reference. Measure foot rotation relative to its support surface; report peak force and torque alongside task success.

16 / Implementation and Hardware

Validate the two contributions separately, then combine

Milestone 1

→
Milestone 2

Planner initialization + navigation RL

→
Milestone 3

Risk control + combined hardware evaluation

First hardware experiment

Repeat partial-foot, low-friction, and foot-twisting contacts with matched tactile and non-tactile controllers. Measure support recovery and falls before testing complex routes.

Integrated demonstration

Use the same humanoid, goal, and course. Sweep risk settings and perturb support conditions. Show both changed contact strategy and recovery quality.

Proceed to deformable or granular terrain only after validating sensor transfer and rigid-terrain recovery.

Start with the available G1 platform. A full-size humanoid demonstration is a later validation target, contingent on platform and sensor access. No experimental results are claimed yet.
