01 / Research Proposal

Risk-conditioned whole-body navigation

Study how dense tactile feedback affects contact recovery and how a risk command changes foothold, hand-support, and route choices.

G1 humanoid · terrain perception · dense six-axis tactile sensing

Research proposal · October 30, 2026
Illustrations describe the proposed method; experiments are planned.

Proposed system: a contact planner selects body–terrain contact targets; a whole-body controller produces joint commands.

Prior work

The attention-based contact planner (ACP) predicts contact targets from terrain observations and a goal. A learned controller executes them without reference motions at deployment.

Our research questions

Does dense tactile sensing reduce foot slip and falls? Can a risk command change the planner’s foothold, hand-support, and route choices?

Terrain perception supplies surface geometry. Tactile sensors measure forces and torques during contact.

ACP · TactileStep · Tac4Loco. Our proposal adds dense six-axis feedback and risk-conditioned planner training.

06 / Contact Command

One target per body for the next 1.5 seconds

Contact command

Each hand, foot, knee, elbow, and the pelvis receives a 3D target. The active flag indicates an expected contact within 1.5 seconds; inactive targets are ignored.

Coordinate frame

The target frame starts at the robot base. Its vertical axis follows gravity; its horizontal axes follow the base heading.

The planner outputs contact positions. Measured forces and torques enter the controller as feedback.

07 / Dense Six-Axis Tactile Feedback

Six-axis tactile measurements

Controller observations

The location and force/torque history of every sensing element, together with base and joint motion. Resolve normal and tangential forces using the contact-surface normal.

What we test

Compare foot slip, foot rotation, recovery time, and falls against pressure-map and total-force/torque baselines.

Force and torque together form a wrench. The sensor does not directly measure slip velocity or the friction limit. Hand feedback requires hand sensors. Force/torque frame conventions.

08 / Stage 1: Contact Controller Training

Train the controller on motion–terrain pairs

Prepare motion–terrain pairs

G1 motion, aligned terrain, and contact labels

→
Train controller with RL

Use contact and motion rewards; apply disturbances

→
Evaluate contact control

Run with contact targets and onboard observations

Training objectives

Reward contact-position accuracy and intended support. Penalize falls, excessive joint effort, and impacts. Use reference-motion rewards during initial training.

Training disturbances

Vary friction, foothold area, pushes, sensor delay, and force/torque noise. Test recovery on rigid terrain before adding compliant supports.

Reference motions provide rewards and initial states during training. The deployed controller receives contact targets and sensor observations.

Check motion–terrain alignment and verify contact labels in dynamic simulation. Replaying joint poses alone does not test balance or contact forces.

09 / Stage 2: Planner Initialization

Label the controller’s successful rollouts

Run the controller

Use reference targets on paired terrains

→
Keep successful episodes

Record when and where body contacts begin

→
Train the contact planner

Predict next contact positions and active flags

Where supervision comes from

In each successful rollout, find the next sustained contact for each body within 1.5 seconds. Use that contact location and active flag as the planner’s training target.

Introduce predicted contacts

For each body, predict probabilities over terrain points and an active flag. Gradually replace reference targets with planner targets while training the controller.

Successful rollouts supply contact labels. Risk training in Stage 3 also uses failed rollouts.

ACP uses this procedure to label contacts from successful controller rollouts. ACP paper, training and hindsight contact labeling (labels extracted after execution).

Training schedule

Train the planner with the controller fixed first. If the controller is later updated, collect new rollouts because contact success rates may change.

Sample targets during training so their action probabilities are available for policy updates. Test whether retaining the contact-supervision loss helps training.

11 / Planner Formulation

Risk changes the policy and objective

A Markov decision process (MDP) models transitions between states. Here, t counts planner updates.

Physical state xₜRobot and terrain state, friction, compliance, controller memory, and remaining time.
Planner action aₜA terrain contact position and active flag for each selected body part.
Observation history hₜTerrain maps, base/joint state, dense tactile signals, and previous actions.
Context g and αg is the goal pose. α is the risk setting, held fixed within an episode.

θ denotes planner parameters; φ denotes fixed controller parameters. Pφ is the transition model induced by the controller and physics.

at∼πθ(·|ht,g,α)；xt+1∼Pφ(·|xt,at)

The augmented task state is sₜ = (xₜ, g, α). Risk changes action selection, not the physics for a fixed action. Hidden friction and compliance make deployment a partially observable Markov decision process (POMDP).

12 / Discounted Return

Return describes one navigation episode

rₜ is the reward at planner step t; γ ∈ (0, 1] is the discount factor; T is the number of steps before success, failure, or timeout.

Gθ,α=∑t=0T−1γtrt

Gθ,α is a random episode return under planner θ and risk setting α. Terrain variation, contact conditions, disturbances, and sampled actions produce different returns.

maxθ𝔼[Gθ,α]

Reward terms

Reward progress and goal completion. Penalize elapsed time, falls, unintended collisions, and joint effort.

Expectation 𝔼

Average over the specified start/terrain distribution and rollout randomness. Use the same task distribution when comparing risk settings.

The discount factor γ controls how later rewards are weighted. It is not the risk command. Keep reward definitions and γ fixed across the risk sweep.

13 / Lower-Tail CVaR

Apply CVaR to the distribution of returns

α ∈ (0, 1] is the fraction of lowest-return episodes to average. This defines lower-tail CVaR.

η is a return threshold optimized below. The positive part (η − G)⁺ = max(η − G, 0) measures how far a return falls below that threshold.

CVaRα↓(Gθ,α)=maxη{η−1α𝔼[(η−Gθ,α)+]}

α = 0.1

Average the worst 10% of returns. This emphasizes episodes with low total navigation reward.

α = 1

Average all returns. The objective reduces to expected-return RL.

CVaR is computed from episode returns. It is not an extra reward added at each simulation step.

This lower-tail convention rewards larger values. It includes risk-averse and risk-neutral objectives. Chow et al., CVaR MDPs.

14 / Chosen RL Objective

Start with CVaR; compare a mean–CVaR objective

Chosen objective, for each risk setting α: optimize the lower tail of the discounted return defined on p12.

maxθCVaRα↓(Gθ,α)

Alternative: let λ ∈ [0, 1] weight CVaR against mean return.

maxθ[(1−λ)𝔼[Gθ,α]+λCVaRα↓(Gθ,α)]

Use λ = 1 for the main method. Vary α as the single user command; test a fixed λ < 1 as an ablation.

Sample α across training episodes and optimize its conditional objective. Retain failed rollouts. A distributional critic estimates returns; the actor update must optimize the chosen risk objective. Ordinary mean-return policy updates are insufficient. Chow & Ghavamzadeh, mean–CVaR optimization.

15 / Constrained MDP Alternative

A constraint requires an explicit cost limit

A constrained Markov decision process (CMDP) adds a cost and an allowed budget. Let cₜ ≥ 0 be a safety cost, Cθ,α its episode total, and b the allowed expected cost.

Cθ,α=∑t=0T−1ct
maxθ𝔼[Gθ,α]subject to𝔼[Cθ,α]≤b

Example: failure budget

Set C = 1 if the robot falls, and 0 otherwise. Then 𝔼[C] equals the fall probability.

Alternative: tail-cost limit

Constrain upper-tail CVaR of C to limit the average cost of the worst episodes. This is a risk-constrained alternative.

Current proposal: risk-sensitive RL without a cost constraint. Add a CMDP only if the task specifies a cost budget b.

The risk setting α is not an allowed failure probability. A fixed cost penalty is not a constraint; an expected-cost bound is not a per-trial safety guarantee. Achiam et al., Constrained Policy Optimization.
